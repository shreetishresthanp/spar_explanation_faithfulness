Finding the ground truth

Follows Harry's debrief suggestion: get a "ground truth" for how each model decides, and use it. We built it in two forms: a fitted rule (in numbers) and plain-English descriptions (in words).

1. Fitted rule (ground truth in numbers)

  • RQ: Can we write down how each model decides?
  • Setup:
  • For each reference model, a simple decision rule (logistic regression) from the scenario's factors to its choices.
  • Factors: law (traffic light), passengers vs. pedestrians, swerving vs. staying in lane, number of lives, the demographic groups (age, gender, status, fitness, species), and answer format (answer asked first or last).
  • Tested on pairs it wasn't fitted on, with each original–counterfactual pair kept together.
  • Finding:
  • The rule predicts the top 3 models' choices (GPT-5.2, Gemini 3 Flash, Gemini 3 Pro) with 92–96% accuracy.
  • GPT-5.2 is driven by law, passengers vs. pedestrians, not swerving, and number of lives.
  • Gemini 3 Flash puts the law first, then humans over animals, group size, and status.
  • Gemma 3 27B's largest factor is answer format, which its explanations never mention.

2. Descriptions of model behaviour (ground truth in words)

  • RQ: Can the rule be turned into an explanation a simulator can use?
  • Setup:
  • For GPT-5.2, Gemini 3 Flash and Gemma 3 27B (control), a short "how this evaluator decides" text.
  • Each was written from training pairs only, so the 100 held-out pairs per model stay unseen.
  • Two versions:
    • Written: by Claude, from the training-only fitted rule and 40 training decisions, then checked claim by claim against training statistics.
    • Automatic: from a small decision tree fitted on the same training decisions, turned into "situation → usual choice" lines. No judgment is involved; this version checks that results don't depend on Claude's writing.
  • Finding:
  • The written descriptions match the fitted rule's main drivers, organised by situation. For example, for GPT-5.2, passengers vs. pedestrians comes first in car scenarios, and law comes first between pedestrian groups.
  • The decision-tree descriptions are coarser. For example, GPT-5.2's tree never mentions passengers vs. pedestrians.
  • Used for: the explanations simulators read in Experiment 1.

Where explanations stop helping

3. Distance to the decision boundary (paper's pairs, observational)

  • RQ: Which counterfactuals carry little explanation signal?
  • Setup: Use the fitted rule to measure how close each counterfactual is to a 50/50 call for the model. Call it a near-tie when the rule's P(Case 1) is between 0.3 and 0.7. Top 3 models, 2,835 pairs.
  • Finding:
  • Too close: counts-only pairs leave no headroom. Predictors are 91% right without the explanation, and NSG is −8%.
  • Near-ties: NSG 12% [2, 20] on near-tie counterfactuals, against 37% [32, 41] on clear ones.
  • ⚠️ The near-tie gap didn't replicate on purpose-built pairs (§6), so treat it as observational, not causal.

4. Hand labels: why GPT-5.2's failures fail

  • RQ: Do predictors fail when the counterfactual changes a factor the explanation doesn't mention?
  • Setup: 30 GPT-5.2 pairs (20 where most predictors failed, 10 where they succeeded), labelled blind to the outcome: which factors the explanation cites, and what it predicts if read literally.
  • Finding:
  • No: citing a factor ≠ covering the counterfactual. Almost every counterfactual changes a cited factor (failures 95%, successes 90%).
  • What separates them:
    • being a near-tie: 60% of failures vs. 10% of successes
    • the explanation pointing the wrong way: 45% vs. 0%. This is partly built in, since the labeller reads the same text as the predictors.
  • The typical failure is a conditional rule whose condition doesn't hold. For example, "protect pedestrians obeying traffic laws" applied to pedestrians crossing on red: all 5 predictors were wrong with the explanation, against 3 of 5 right without it.
  • 8 of 20 failures are explanations that can't decide the counterfactual, e.g. "minimise deaths" on a pair with equal deaths.
  • Only 3 of 20 are explanations that contradict what the fitted rule says the model consistently does. These are the strongest unfaithfulness candidates, and one of the three is itself a near-tie.

Experiments

5. Experiment 1: does a description of the model's behaviour beat its own explanation?

  • RQ: If a simulator reads a description of how the model actually decides, does it predict better than with the model's own explanation? If so, the explanation is missing information.
  • Setup:
  • GPT-5.2, Gemini 3 Flash, and Gemma 3 27B as the control. 100 held-out pairs each, from the paper's pairs.
  • 2 simulators: gpt-oss-20b and Gemini 3 Flash.
  • 5 conditions: none, own explanation, written description, automatic description, written + own.
  • 3,000 calls.
  • Finding:
  • Gemma: the description exposes what its explanation hides. Accuracy is 64% with its own explanation (worse than 72% with none) and 92% with the description: NSG −29% → +71%. The automatic description also beats its own explanation, so this doesn't depend on Claude's writing.
  • GPT-5.2 and Gemini 3 Flash: no evidence a description adds anything. Their own explanations do as well or better (89–91% vs. 83–84%).
  • Simulators don't fully apply a written rule: 83–92% with it, against 88–96% for the rule itself. So "the explanation recovers half of the rule's gain" measured reader vs. fitted model, not missing information.

6. Experiment 2: purpose-built counterfactuals

  • RQ: On counterfactuals chosen on purpose, is explanation gain lower near the model's decision boundary?
  • Setup:
  • New pairs built from scenarios each model already answered, so no new reference-model calls.
  • Per model: 50 near-tie + 50 clear pairs, each changing exactly 1 decision factor. Half are flips; traffic-light changes are capped at half.
  • Own explanation vs. none; 2 simulators × 2 repeats; 3,000 calls.
  • Gemma arm: 50 extra pairs from held-out scenarios only, adding the description as a third condition.
  • Finding:
  • The near-tie gap didn't replicate. NSG is 21% on near-tie vs. 22% on clear pairs (GPT-5.2 + Gemini 3 Flash; difference −1 point [−24, +22]). Near-ties are harder with or without the explanation (65% vs. 76% accuracy), but the explanation helps about as much.
  • It isn't a flip-rate effect (31.5% vs. 30.8% on the paper's pairs). Why the two sets of pairs differ is still open.
  • Cited vs. uncited is confounded: cited pairs flip far more often (75% vs. 29%), so it can't be read.
  • Gemma's description still helps on new pairs: 71% with its own explanation, 74% with none, 82% with the description (NSG −11% → +30% [+7, +56]).

7. Gemma answer-format test (fresh runs)

  • RQ: Does the answer format cause Gemma's choice, and does it hold in fresh runs? (The debrief to-do.)
  • Setup:
  • 100 held-out scenarios, each asked answer-first and answer-last, 3 samples each. Fresh Gemma 3 27B calls with the paper's sampling settings.
  • The two prompts differ only in where [ANSWER] is requested.
  • Control: Gemini 3 Flash. Calibration: 20 of the paper's prompts, unchanged.
  • 860 calls.
  • Finding:
  • Causal and reproducible. On the same scenarios, Gemma picks Case 1 51% of the time when asked first and 94% when asked last.
  • 44% of scenarios flip with the format alone, all towards Case 1 when answering last. Gemini 3 Flash: 0%.
  • Beyond noise: the same prompt twice disagrees 10% of the time; across formats, 43%.
  • Fresh answers match the paper's stored answers on 93% of the unchanged prompts.
  • Explanations still argue from content. In scenario 341, "prioritizing the safety of those adhering to the law" is used to justify the answer that kills the lawful crossers (asked last); asked first, Gemma gave the opposite answer for the same reason.
  • Consistency (the debrief's question): 83–88% of scenarios give the same answer on all 3 samples.
  • Parser note: asked first, Gemma often omits the [ANSWER] tag. The repo parser drops those (mostly Case 2), inflating the answer-first rate. Strict parsing gives 65% vs. 97%: still a large effect.

8. What changed since the debrief

At the debrief Now
Low NSG may mean counterfactuals are "too far away" Not raw distance: counts-only pairs are too close, and the near-tie effect didn't replicate
Build counterfactuals from the factors explanations cite Explanations already cite almost everything. Citing ≠ covering: conditional rules fail outside their condition
Frontier explanations recover about half of what's predictable That compared a fitted model with a reader. A behaviour description doesn't beat their own explanations
Gemma looks unfaithful (position bias) Causal and reproducible: the same scenario flips with the answer format alone. Simulators confirm it 3 ways

9. Limitations

  • Small samples: 30 hand labels (one labeller); 100 pairs per model in the experiments. Intervals are wide.
  • Simulator noise: the same prompt twice agrees 84–98% of the time, and one frontier result moved 25 NSG points between two runs.
  • Self-simulation: Gemini 3 Flash is both a reference model and a simulator.
  • 2 simulators, not the paper's 5, and stratified samples: compare within each experiment, not with the paper's NSG.
  • Main effects only: the fitted rule has no interactions, so where it's wrong the cause could be noise or a missing interaction.
  • Cost: $3.63 of the $50 key.
