Self-Explanation Faithfulness: Metrics and Training
Abstract
Normalised simulatability gain (NSG) is a metric that measures an LLM’s faithfulness by how much it helps a second model (a “simulator”) predict the LLM’s behavior in similar situations, given a decision it made and its corresponding explanation. This setup attributes every shortfall to an incompleteness of the LLM’s own explanation, but in practice, exogenous factors may also influence NSG. We test several such factors in toy settings with a known decision rule, which lets us isolate simulator capability, explanation specificity, counterfactual distance, and reference non-determinism. 1. Introduction
Language models are often asked to make decisions and then to explain them. A model is faithful if its explanation correctly reports the reason for its decision. One way to operationalise “correctly” is predictive: a correct explanation should help a reader predict how the model will behave on a similar problem [3, 4]. Mayne et al. [1] turn this into a metric, normalised simulatability gain (NSG): a simulator model sees a reference model’s decision and explanation and predicts the reference model’s decision on a nearby counterfactual, and an explanation that fails to raise the simulator’s accuracy is scored as unfaithful.
However, this setup assumes that the simulator must be a perfect executor, basing its prediction on the information in the explanation and nothing else. Besides unfaithfulness (1), a simulator may fail through (2) capability, when the simulator isn’t robust enough to apply a complex explanation; (3) counterfactual distance, when the counterfactual lies too far from the reference for the explanation to entail its answer; and (4) reference non-determinism, when the reference does not decide the same way twice. We replace the reference model with a known rule, which fixes each failure mode at a known value and lets us measure its share of the NSG shortfall.
2. Methods
We reverse Mayne et al.’s setup. Instead of a reference models, fixed decisions and explanations are generated from a deterministic rule  over toy features (loan approval, triage, hiring, simple math expressions). Explanations are from  in code, so every explanation is faithful by construction. Thus any observed NSG loss belongs to the simulator or the counterfactual by construction.
We test simulator capability, the ability to apply a complete explanation, in two setups. (1) benchmarking five simulators on 20 LLM-generated rules at each of three difficulty tiers. (2) Varying seven information-equivalent renderings of one same rule (explanation format).
We then test capability under known information loss, asking whether an explanation that is % incomplete yields a simulator that is % less accurate.  would mean the simulator degrades faster than its input;  would imply that it fills gaps from its own priors and overstates the explanation’s own effectiveness. In setup (3), we vary content on triage rules of three to five conditions: soundness (faithful, one condition omitted, one substituted), specificity (exact thresholds to feature names only, P0–P3), and prior alignment (intuitive, mirrored, abstract), on nine simulators. In setup (4), we remove 4, 12, or 24 nodes from a 128-leaf rule, and evaluate how simulator models react on out-of-explanation counterfactuals, with and without a sentence stating that the explanation is incomplete.

3. Results
Simulator capability (setups 1 and 2). Given a complete and precisely stated rule, every reasoning simulator we tested predicts counterfactual decisions at or near the ceiling, and raising the difficulty tier does not separate them; the only models that fall short are non-reasoning ones, which fail even when handed exact thresholds (Fig. 1). How the rule is written matters only at the extremes. Through rules of about ten clauses, the seven information-equivalent renderings are read identically; only at thirty-two clauses does dense code, and above all its negated form, cost a small open-weight simulator accuracy that structured English preserves and that a larger model largely recovers (Fig. 2). No rule was found that breaks gpt-5-mini, and no format separates simulators at ordinary rule sizes.
Fig. 1. Setup 1: NSG by simulator across three difficulty tiers of complete rules (20 boundaries × 5 items per tier; 95% bootstrap intervals). Reasoning simulators sit at the ceiling at every tier; Gemma 3 27B does not.
Fig. 2. Setup 2: accuracy of gpt-oss-20b and gpt-oss-120b on identical 32-clause rules presented in seven information-equivalent formats (n = 96 per cell). Structured English is read near-perfectly; code and negated code cost the smaller model most; the larger model narrows the spread.
Known information loss (setups 3 and 4). What degrades simulation is the content of the explanation, not the complexity of the rule. As a faithful explanation moves from exact thresholds to approximate values, to qualitative words, to feature names alone, the simulator’s gain collapses, until a vague faithful explanation scores no better than a precise unfaithful one (Fig. 3). A frontier simulator recovers a few points at the qualitative level, where its remaining errors are irreducible ambiguities of wording, and nothing at the feature-name level, where only priors remain (Fig. 4). When conditions are deleted outright, frontier simulators lose accuracy in exact proportion to the information withheld: Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5 sit on the literal-reader ceiling at every dose, follow the stated rule on every item, and score zero on items decided by a removed condition whether or not that condition matched loan intuition; a sentence warning that the explanation is incomplete lengthens their reasoning and changes nothing (Fig. 5). Read together, the two setups suggest that simulators resolve ambiguity about stated features with priors and treat unstated conditions as absent. Setup 3 also exposes a property of the metric rather than of the simulator: the same one-condition omission earns about twice the NSG on mirrored, counter-intuitive rules as on intuitive ones, with identical with-explanation accuracy, because the simulator’s no-explanation baseline is lower when the rule runs against its priors (Fig. 6).
Fig. 3. Setup 3: NSG of faithful, omission, and substitution explanations as specificity falls from exact thresholds (P0) to feature names only (P3), gpt-oss-20b, 95% bootstrap bands. Faithful NSG collapses with vagueness and the faithful–unsound margin shrinks.
Fig. 4. Setup 3: accuracy on faithful explanations at the qualitative (P2) and feature-only (P3) levels for six simulators, by rule type, with cost per 1,000 requests. Frontier models gain a few points at P2 and nothing at P3; Gemini 3.8 Flash matches GPT-6 Astra.
Fig. 5. Setup 4: accuracy of Claude Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5 against the number of conditions removed from the explanation, silent and flagged, with the literal-reader and hedging ceilings. Both simulators sit on the literal ceiling at every dose.
Fig. 6. Setup 3: the same one-condition omission on intuitive versus mirrored (counter-intuitive) rules. With-explanation accuracy is unchanged, the no-explanation baseline falls, and NSG roughly doubles.

4. Discussion
Reasoning simulators (particularly at the frontier) seem to be reliable instruments. On explanations of ordinary size their error is near zero, and under known omission their accuracy falls in exact proportion to the information withheld, so an NSG deficit measured with a capable simulator can be read as information missing from the explanation, i.e, a loss of faithfulness. However, non-reasoning models are slightly less capable, occasionally fail even on exact rules, and no simulator perfectly recovers a threshold that an explanation states only in words.
A second qualification concerns the baseline in NSG’s denominator. Because the metric scores an explanation by the share of the remaining headroom it closes, the same explanation earns about twice the NSG on a rule the simulator cannot guess as on one it can (Fig. 6), with identical with-explanation accuracy. NSG therefore rewards decisions for being surprising as well as explanations for being faithful, and a model trained against it could raise its score by becoming less predictable without an explanation rather than by explaining itself better.

5. Conclusion
Toy models with known rules separate simulator error, counterfactual coverage, and non-determinism from faithfulness. Current reasoning models clear the capability floor and track the information in an explanation exactly. What NSG then measures is specificity, coverage, consistency, and the baseline as much as faithfulness, and it should be read with those alongside, especially before it is used as a reward.
